\doxysubsection{Detailed Description}
API for the geodesic routines in C. 

This an implementation in C of the geodesic algorithms described in
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item C. F. F. Karney, \href{https://doi.org/10.1007/s00190-012-0578-z}{\texttt{Algorithms for geodesics}}, J. Geodesy {\bfseries{87}}, 43--55 (2013); DOI:\+ \href{https://doi.org/10.1007/s00190-012-0578-z}{\texttt{10.\+1007/\+s00190-\/012-\/0578-\/z}}; addenda:\+ \href{https://geographiclib.sourceforge.io/geod-addenda.html}{\texttt{geod-\/addenda.\+html}}.
\end{DoxyItemize}\doxylink{requirements_The}{The} principal advantages of these algorithms over previous ones (e.\+g., Vincenty, 1975) are
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item accurate to round off for \texorpdfstring{$\vert$}{|}{\itshape f}\texorpdfstring{$\vert$}{|} \texorpdfstring{$<$}{<} 1/\+50;
\item the solution of the inverse problem is always found;
\item differential and integral properties of geodesics are computed.
\end{DoxyItemize}

\doxylink{requirements_The}{The} shortest path between two points on the ellipsoid at ({\itshape lat1}, {\itshape lon1}) and ({\itshape lat2}, {\itshape lon2}) is called the geodesic. Its length is {\itshape s12} and the geodesic from point 1 to point 2 has forward azimuths {\itshape azi1} and {\itshape azi2} at the two end points.

Traditionally two geodesic problems are considered:\+
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item the direct problem -- given {\itshape lat1}, {\itshape lon1}, {\itshape s12}, and {\itshape azi1}, determine {\itshape lat2}, {\itshape lon2}, and {\itshape azi2}. This is solved by the function \doxylink{geodesic_8h_ac9ec3020fe7b74de822d83da358215f3}{geod\+\_\+direct()}.
\item the inverse problem -- given {\itshape lat1}, {\itshape lon1}, and {\itshape lat2}, {\itshape lon2}, determine {\itshape s12}, {\itshape azi1}, and {\itshape azi2}. This is solved by the function \doxylink{geodesic_8h_adcc4c0c327d3bc230c3cd429550f104d}{geod\+\_\+inverse()}.
\end{DoxyItemize}

\doxylink{requirements_The}{The} ellipsoid is specified by its equatorial radius {\itshape a} (typically in meters) and flattening {\itshape f}. \doxylink{requirements_The}{The} routines are accurate to round off with double precision arithmetic provided that \texorpdfstring{$\vert$}{|}{\itshape f}\texorpdfstring{$\vert$}{|} \texorpdfstring{$<$}{<} 1/\+50; for the WGS84 ellipsoid, the errors are less than 15 nanometers. (Reasonably accurate results are obtained for \texorpdfstring{$\vert$}{|}{\itshape f}\texorpdfstring{$\vert$}{|} \texorpdfstring{$<$}{<} 1/\+5.) For a prolate ellipsoid, specify {\itshape f} \texorpdfstring{$<$}{<} 0.

\doxylink{requirements_The}{The} routines also calculate several other quantities of interest
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item {\itshape S12} is the area between the geodesic from point 1 to point 2 and the equator; i.\+e., it is the area, measured counter-\/clockwise, of the quadrilateral with corners ({\itshape lat1},{\itshape lon1}), (0,{\itshape lon1}), (0,{\itshape lon2}), and ({\itshape lat2},{\itshape lon2}).
\item {\itshape m12}, the reduced length of the geodesic is defined such that if the initial azimuth is perturbed by {\itshape dazi1} (radians) then the second point is displaced by {\itshape m12} {\itshape dazi1} in the direction perpendicular to the geodesic. On a curved surface the reduced length obeys a symmetry relation, {\itshape m12} + {\itshape m21} = 0. On a flat surface, we have {\itshape m12} = {\itshape s12}.
\item {\itshape M12} and {\itshape M21} are geodesic scales. If two geodesics are parallel at point 1 and separated by a small distance {\itshape dt}, then they are separated by a distance {\itshape M12} {\itshape dt} at point 2. {\itshape M21} is defined similarly (with the geodesics being parallel to one another at point 2). On a flat surface, we have {\itshape M12} = {\itshape M21} = 1.
\item {\itshape a12} is the arc length on the auxiliary sphere. This is a construct for converting the problem to one in spherical trigonometry. {\itshape a12} is measured in degrees. \doxylink{requirements_The}{The} spherical arc length from one equator crossing to the next is always 180\textdegree{}.
\end{DoxyItemize}

If points 1, 2, and 3 lie on a single geodesic, then the following addition rules hold:\+
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item {\itshape s13} = {\itshape s12} + {\itshape s23} 
\item {\itshape a13} = {\itshape a12} + {\itshape a23} 
\item {\itshape S13} = {\itshape S12} + {\itshape S23} 
\item {\itshape m13} = {\itshape m12} {\itshape M23} + {\itshape m23} {\itshape M21} 
\item {\itshape M13} = {\itshape M12} {\itshape M23} - (1 - {\itshape M12} {\itshape M21}) {\itshape m23} /\+ {\itshape m12} 
\item {\itshape M31} = {\itshape M32} {\itshape M21} - (1 - {\itshape M23} {\itshape M32}) {\itshape m12} /\+ {\itshape m23} 
\end{DoxyItemize}

\doxylink{requirements_The}{The} shortest distance returned by the solution of the inverse problem is (obviously) uniquely defined. However, in a few special cases there are multiple azimuths which yield the same shortest distance. Here is a catalog of those cases:\+
\begin{DoxyItemize}
\item {\itshape lat1} = -{\itshape lat2} (with neither point at a pole). If {\itshape azi1} = {\itshape azi2}, the geodesic is unique. Otherwise there are two geodesics and the second one is obtained by setting \+[{\itshape azi1}, {\itshape azi2}]\+ {$\rightarrow$} \+[{\itshape azi2}, {\itshape azi1}]\+, \+[{\itshape M12}, {\itshape M21}]\+ {$\rightarrow$} \+[{\itshape M21}, {\itshape M12}]\+, {\itshape S12} {$\rightarrow$} -{\itshape S12}. (This occurs when the longitude difference is near {$\pm$}180\textdegree{} for oblate ellipsoids.)
\item {\itshape lon2} = {\itshape lon1} {$\pm$} 180\textdegree{} (with neither point at a pole). If {\itshape azi1} = 0\textdegree{} or {$\pm$}180\textdegree{}, the geodesic is unique. Otherwise there are two geodesics and the second one is obtained by setting \+[{\itshape azi1}, {\itshape azi2}]\+ {$\rightarrow$} \+[-{\itshape azi1}, -{\itshape azi2}]\+, {\itshape S12} {$\rightarrow$} -{\itshape S12}. (This occurs when {\itshape lat2} is near -{\itshape lat1} for prolate ellipsoids.)
\item Points 1 and 2 at opposite poles. There are infinitely many geodesics which can be generated by setting \+[{\itshape azi1}, {\itshape azi2}]\+ {$\rightarrow$} \+[{\itshape azi1}, {\itshape azi2}]\+ + \+[{\itshape d}, -{\itshape d}]\+, for arbitrary {\itshape d}. (For spheres, this prescription applies when points 1 and 2 are antipodal.)
\item {\itshape s12} = 0 (coincident points). There are infinitely many geodesics which can be generated by setting \+[{\itshape azi1}, {\itshape azi2}]\+ {$\rightarrow$} \+[{\itshape azi1}, {\itshape azi2}]\+ + \+[{\itshape d}, {\itshape d}]\+, for arbitrary {\itshape d}.
\end{DoxyItemize}
